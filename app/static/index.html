Remote photoplethysmography

Is there a heartbeat in this face?

Each heartbeat pushes blood under the skin, so for a moment the skin reflects a little less green light. A camera can record that change. PulseCheck reads the pulse from ordinary video, then checks whether every patch of the face shares the same one.

For a clean pulse

  • Face a window or a lamp so your face is lit evenly, with no flicker.
  • Fill the oval, then keep still and breathe normally.
  • Take glasses off if they reflect, and move hair off your forehead.
  • We record at a high bit rate, because compression wipes out the faint colour change.

The camera video never leaves your browser until you press Analyze.

Videos are processed in memory and deleted when the analysis finishes. Nothing is stored.

How it works

From pixels to a pulse

Six steps, from a video file to a heart rate and a real-or-fake score. The lab below runs the middle of the pipeline live in your browser, so you can watch each step change.

Find the face

MediaPipe Face Landmarker places 478 points on the face in every frame. Six skin patches are drawn between fixed points: two forehead halves, two cheeks, the nose bridge and the chin. A colour model learned from each video's own cheeks drops hair and eyebrow pixels.

One number per frame

Each patch's pixels are averaged into one red, one green and one blue value per frame. A 10 second clip at 30 frames a second becomes 300 numbers per colour, and those numbers are all the rest of the pipeline sees.

Separate the pulse from the noise

Lighting changes, movement and compression are usually bigger than the pulse. POS (Wang et al., 2017) combines the three colours so brightness changes cancel out. A band-pass filter then keeps only 0.7 to 3 Hz, the range where a heartbeat lives (42 to 180 beats a minute).

Read the beats

A Fourier transform splits the signal into its frequencies. The strongest one in the band, times 60, is the heart rate. In a 10 second clip the frequency steps are 0.1 Hz apart, which is 6 beats a minute.

Compare the patches

On a real face every patch is fed by the same heart, so their pulses rise and fall together. For each 8 second window, 91 numbers describe how well the patches agree: coherence, correlation, lag, and how strongly each patch shows the shared rhythm.

Decide, then test honestly

Two detectors turn each window into a probability of being fake: a classical model on those numbers, and a small CNN that reads a "PPG map" (32 patches' pulses stacked over time) and was trained on a laptop GPU. The windows vote, and the site uses whichever detector did better on validation data. Both are tested only on people they never saw, then on a different dataset entirely. See Results.

Where it goes wrong

Compression

Video compression throws away exactly the small colour changes the pulse lives in. The Results page measures heart-rate error at several H.264 quality levels.

Movement and uneven light

These cause most false alarms. The app warns you when the face moves a lot or the pulse signal is weak.

Short clips

A 10 second clip can only tell beats apart in 6 per minute steps; a 30 second clip, 2. The lab above shows why.

Testing on people you trained on

Clips of one person look alike, so a random split flatters the score. Results shows the same model scored both ways.

Skin tone

Heart-rate readers lose signal on darker skin, where melanin absorbs more of the light. Results breaks error down by an estimated skin-tone angle (ITA), and says when a dataset is too narrow to tell.

Good fakes can have a heart

Since 2025, high-quality face swaps have been shown to carry over the pulse of the video they were made from. A believable pulse is evidence, not proof.

About

Model card

What this is, what it was trained on, and when not to trust it.

What it is

A learning project that implements remote photoplethysmography (rPPG) and a FakeCatcher-style consistency check, after Ciftci, Demir and Yin (2020). It is not a forensic tool. Do not use it to accuse anyone of anything.

Pipeline

MediaPipe Face Landmarker (478 points), then six skin patches and a 32-patch grid, then POS, CHROM or green rPPG, then a 0.7 to 3 Hz band-pass and an FFT. Detection has two models, both trained locally: a scikit-learn classifier on 91 cross-patch features per 8 second window (chosen by grouped cross-validation), and a CNN on PPG maps (PyTorch, trained on an NVIDIA RTX 3050). The site serves whichever scored higher on validation, and a video's score is the mean of its window votes.

Data

  • UBFC-rPPG: 42 uncompressed webcam videos with a finger pulse oximeter, used for heart-rate error.
  • FaceForensics++ (c23): 1,000 YouTube videos and five manipulation methods, with the official train/val/test split by source pair.
  • Celeb-DF v2: the 518-video test list, never trained on, used for cross-dataset testing.

None of these datasets are redistributed here. Each needs its owners' terms to be accepted.

Known limits

  • Expect well below the 96% reported by FakeCatcher on its easiest test set. That paper reports about 91% on its own harder set.
  • High-quality face swaps can inherit the source video's pulse (Seibold et al., 2025).
  • Compression, motion, low light and makeup weaken the signal. Darker skin tones are under-represented in UBFC-rPPG.
  • Webcam clips look different from the YouTube-style training videos, so the verdict on your own clip is less certain than the test numbers suggest.

Privacy

Uploaded or recorded videos are written to a temporary file, analysed, and deleted. No video, frame or result is stored or logged.
